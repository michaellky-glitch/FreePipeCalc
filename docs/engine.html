Calculation Method

How FreePipeCalc calculates friction loss, solves a network, handles equipment and thermal, and sizes domestic water pipework.

Every equation here can be checked by hand. Worked examples show the arithmetic.

1. Scope

This document tells you what the program calculates and how. It does not tell you how to use the interface.

The program calculates:

  • Friction loss in each pipe and fitting.
  • Flow and pressure at every point in a network, including loops.
  • Equipment duty and pipe heat loss in a thermal network.
  • Pipe sizes for domestic water, from fixture units.
IMPORTANT:

No certification body or Authority Having Jurisdiction has approved these results. A qualified engineer must check them. The program neglects velocity pressure, so do not use it for fire protection design.

3. Pipe friction

Two methods are available. Select the method on the HYDRAULIC tab. The calculation sheet records which method you used.

3.1 Hazen-Williams (default)

Hazen-Williams is an empirical correlation for turbulent water flow. It is the usual method for building-services pipework.

The program uses the SI velocity form printed in the 2021 ASHRAE Handbook — Fundamentals, Chapter 22, Equation (6):

Friction head loss — velocity form (ASHRAE) hf = 6.819 · L · (V/C)1.852 · (1/d)1.167

The solver works with flow, not velocity. The substitution V = 4Q / (π d²) gives the flow form:

Friction head loss — flow form (derived) A = 6.819 × (4/π)1.852 = 10.6663
e = 1.167 + 2 × 1.852        = 4.8710

hf = 10.6663 · L_eff · Q1.852 / ( C1.852 · d4.8710 )

The effective length L_eff includes the fitting equivalent lengths (section 4.1). The three printed constants (6.819, 1.852, 1.167) are editable on the HYDRAULIC tab. The program derives the flow-form coefficients from them.

NOTE:

The same equation is often printed with the rounded constants 10.67 and 4.8704. The two forms agree to 0.15%. The difference is the rounding in 10.67, not an error in either form.

How the solver stores it

The solver stores each pipe as a resistance r and an exponent n. The loss is a power law:

hf = r · |Q|n−1 · Q

r = 10.6663 · L_eff / ( C1.852 · d4.8710 )     n = 1.852

The loss is written signed in Q, not as |Q|n. This makes it an odd function: reverse the flow and the loss reverses with it. The solver can then reverse the flow in a loop without any special case.

SUGGESTION:

100 m of DN50 Schedule 40 pipe. Bore 52.48 mm, C = 120, flow 5 L/s.

r = 10.6663 × 100 / (1201.852 × 0.052484.8710) = 258 392.9
hf = 258 392.9 × 0.0051.852 = 14.15 m
ΔP = 998 × 9.81 × 14.15 = 138.5 kPa
V = 0.005 / (π × 0.05248² / 4) = 2.31 m/s

The test suite asserts this case and expects 14.1505 m (test/engine.test.js).

3.2 Darcy-Weisbach

Darcy-Weisbach applies to any fluid and any flow regime. It uses absolute roughness ε instead of a C value.

Friction head loss hf = f · (L_eff / d) · V² / 2g

As a solver resistance:  r = 8 · f · L_eff / (π² · g · d⁵),   n = 2

The friction factor f depends on the Reynolds number, so r is not constant. The program recalculates r between solver passes from the flow of the previous pass. The first pass assumes 1 m/s.

Four correlations are available. Colebrook-White is the default.

CorrelationFormAgreement with Colebrook-White
Colebrook-White (default)ImplicitThe reference the other three are fitted to. Solved by iteration, as shown in section 3.3.
Swamee-JainExplicitMeasured in the test suite against an iterated Colebrook-White: within 0.9% for 10⁴ ≤ Re ≤ 10⁷ and ε/d ≤ 10⁻³. The error rises to 2.8% at Re = 5×10³ with ε/d = 10⁻², the corner of its published validity.
HaalandExplicitWithin about 2%. The simplest form.
ChurchillExplicitCovers all flow regimes in one expression.

The choice of correlation has little effect on the answer. Across realistic building-services cases (DN25 to DN200, new to heavily fouled pipe) the four correlations agree to about 2%.

Laminar flow uses f = 64/Re. In the transitional band the program blends linearly between the laminar and turbulent values, so the solver sees a continuous curve and not a step. Churchill covers all regimes itself and is not blended.

3.4 Which method to use

NOTE:

Against Hazen-Williams at C = 120, Darcy-Weisbach with ε = 0.045 mm gives a friction rate roughly 15–30% lower (DN25 to DN200 at 1–2.5 m/s).

Neither is wrong. C = 120 represents somewhat aged steel. ε = 0.045 mm represents clean new steel. To compare the two methods, first make them equivalent: use ε ≈ 0.15–0.25 mm, or C ≈ 130–140.

  • Use Hazen-Williams for water at normal building temperatures. This is the default.
  • Use Darcy-Weisbach for other fluids, for glycol mixtures, or where flow is laminar.

3.5 Flow regime

After the solution settles, the program calculates the Reynolds number for each section:

Re = V · d / ν
Reynolds numberRegimeWhat the program does
Re < 2300LaminarWarns. Hazen-Williams does not apply here: it is fitted to turbulent water flow, so in laminar flow it is the wrong equation, not an imprecise one. Use Darcy-Weisbach.
2300 ≤ Re < 4000TransitionalWarns. Friction loss in this band is inherently uncertain.
Re ≥ 4000TurbulentNo warning.

Laminar sections usually appear on oversized pipe at low demand, or on a branch that is nearly shut. You can switch this check off on the HYDRAULIC tab.

NOTE:

A pipe whose flow prints as 0.00 L/s raises no regime warning. A dead leg, or a branch behind a shut valve, carries a few thousandths of a litre a second and would otherwise be reported as laminar against a figure the sheet shows as nothing. The warning exists to flag an unreliable friction loss, and the loss through a pipe carrying nothing is nothing.

3.6 Very low flow

At Q = 0 the slope of the loss curve is zero: the slope is n·r·|Q|n−1, which falls to zero as Q approaches zero for any n above 1. The solver divides by that slope, so it cannot use the curve at the origin.

Below Q_MIN = 1×10⁻⁶ m³/s the program replaces the curve with its tangent at Q_MIN. The tangent passes through the origin. The loss stays continuous, odd, and increasing. The slope stays finite.

4. Fittings

You do not place fittings. The program finds them from the geometry of the drawing. It counts the pipes that meet at each node, and it measures the angles between them. The program adds the fitting loss to the pipe that carries the flow away from the fitting. Section 4.3 gives the rule for tees.

Each fitting is charged as an extra length of straight pipe. The equivalent length comes from a published table, selected on the HYDRAULIC tab. It is not calculated from an L/D ratio.

Mapping one number onto two would be an interpretation, so Table 7 is not used.

4.3 How a tee is charged

The direction of the flow sets which leg is the "run" and which is the "branch". The program knows the direction only after the solve. It therefore does three steps:

  1. It solves with a geometric estimate. The straightest pair of legs is the run.
  2. It sets the run and the branch again from the solved flow directions.
  3. It solves again.

This settles in one or two passes. If the assignment does not settle, the program keeps the last solution and gives a warning.

The charge follows the flow. Each stream pays for its own passage through the fitting:

  • Dividing tee (one pipe in, two out): the program charges both outlets.
  • Combining tee (two pipes in, one out): the program charges both inlets. In a combining tee the branch inflow has most of the loss. A charge on the outlet only would be too small.
  • Elbow: the program charges the downstream pipe.

Two special cases keep the assignment stable and correct:

  • Ties break on geometry. When two legs carry almost the same flow, the straightest in-and-out pair becomes the run. Geometry does not depend on the solution, so the assignment cannot move. At a riser tee, this makes the vertical run the "run" and the floor take-off the "branch".
  • A symmetric split has no run. The two charged legs leave the common leg at equal angles: a bullhead tee, or a symmetric Y. The program charges both legs as a branch. Both streams turn, and the branch coefficient is for a stream that turns.

The program classifies elbows by the angle between the two pipes. It measures the angle as the deviation from straight:

DeviationFitting charged
less than 8°None — effectively straight
8° to 75°45° elbow
more than 75°90° elbow

Valves are not in these tables. The program models a valve by a flow coefficient (section 11).

4.4 The branch coefficient varies with the flow ratio

A tee is the largest approximation in this program. The tables in sections 4.1 and 4.2 give one number for a tee branch at all flows. But the true coefficient changes with the division of the flow. It changes by more than a factor of ten from one end of the range to the other. A branch that takes 5% of the main is not the same fitting as a branch that takes 90% of it.

With Darcy-Weisbach, the program therefore reads the branch coefficient from a curve, and not from a table row. The run keeps its constant value. Hazen-Williams does not change. It keeps the equivalent lengths in section 4.1.

The reference velocity

Idelchik gives these coefficients against the velocity in the common leg. He does not give them against the velocity in the leg that the program charges:

Δh = ζc · Vc² / 2g

The program charges a fitting to one pipe, at that pipe's own velocity. It therefore converts the coefficient:

Into the frame of the charged leg Kb = ζc · (Vc / Vb)² = ζc · (Qc / Qb)² · (Fb / Fc)²
IMPORTANT:

The conversion divides by the flow ratio. Thus the coefficient increases without limit as the branch flow decreases to zero. The head loss stays small, but the solver cannot use a resistance of that size. The program therefore holds the conversion at Qb/Qc = 0.1. This is the first column of the table, and it is where the published data stops. The program charges a branch that carries less than a tenth of the combined flow as if it carried a tenth. The true loss is smaller, thus the result is on the safe side.

SUGGESTION:

The conversion, and not the curve, causes most of the change on a large model. A riser that enters the middle of a header is the usual example. The riser is small and fast, and the header legs are large and slow. The loss is a function of the fast riser velocity, but the older method charged it at the slow header velocity, thus the loss was much too small. On one example, a DN40 riser at 3.5 m/s divides into a DN200 header. The two header legs were charged 1.2 mm of head in total. They are now charged 1.68 m, which is 2.7 velocity heads of the riser. This agrees with the published value for a bullhead tee. Most other branches are charged less than before, and not more.

When the coefficient is calculated

The coefficient depends on the flow, but the flow is the unknown that the solver must find. The program breaks this loop in the same way that it finds the run (section 4.3). It takes the flow ratio from the previous pass, and it holds that ratio for all of the next pass. It calculates the ratio again between passes, but not during a pass.

IMPORTANT:

This changes Darcy-Weisbach results for each model that has tees. It does not change Hazen-Williams results. On a fourteen-terminal example, the total flow changed by 0.5%, the flow in one branch changed by 1.4%, and the index circuit changed. Examine the index circuit again after you change to this version.

6. Network solution

6.1 What the solver does

A branched network can be solved by adding losses along each path. A network with a loop cannot. The flow divides between two routes, and how it divides is itself part of the answer.

The program uses the Global Gradient Algorithm (Todini–Pilati). This is the method EPANET uses. It solves for two sets of unknowns at the same time:

  • the head H at every junction,
  • the flow Q in every link.

Flow is positive in the direction from the from node to the to node. A negative result means the water flows the other way.

6.2 The equations

Two conditions must hold together.

1 — Continuity, at every junction Σ Qin − Σ Qout = demand at the node
2 — Energy, along every link Hfrom − Hto = hf(Q) = r · |Q|n−1 · Q

The second equation is not linear. The solver linearises it. At the current estimate of Q it takes the slope of the loss curve:

Slope of the loss curve dhf/dQ = n · r · |Q|n−1

It then builds one linear system for all the heads, solves it, and updates every flow. The matrix is a graph Laplacian: symmetric, positive definite, with about four non-zero entries per row. The program factorises it as a skyline LDLᵀ, which suits that shape. A dense solver with partial pivoting is the fallback for any matrix that turns out not to be positive definite.

Convergence

The solver repeats until three tests all pass:

  • the largest head change is below 1 mm,
  • the largest nodal flow imbalance is below 0.01 L/s,
  • the largest flow change is below 10⁻⁸ m³/s, or below 0.01% of the largest flow in the network.

The third test matters in a closed loop. A flow circulating around a loop satisfies continuity exactly and perturbs no head, so the first two tests alone would accept a phantom circulation left over from the initial guess. The iteration cap is 100. The solver reports the number of iterations and the remaining imbalance. Convergence is fast because each step is a Newton step: near the answer, the error falls quadratically.

IMPORTANT:

A solution can converge and still describe a system you did not intend. A ring main that is not actually connected as a ring returns zero flow everywhere. It converges with no errors. The program therefore checks the drawing separately for breaks, islands, and unconnected devices.

6.3 Worked example

This example is small enough to check by hand. The file is examples/GGA example.json.

A source feeds a pump. The pump feeds a ring. Three outflows are spaced around one side of the ring. Each draws 1 L/s and requires 100 kPa. All pipe is DN50, C = 120, and the equivalent-length table is the default (Carrier).

Step 1 — resistance of each pipe

Each pipe gets an r from its effective length. The tee at the pump discharge is a bullhead: the supply arrives horizontally and both legs of the ring leave vertically, at equal angles. Both legs are charged as branch (section 4.3). The DN50 branch value in the Carrier table is 10 ft, converted to 3.05 m. Each 90° elbow adds the DN50 elbow value, 5 ft = 1.52 m. Step 2 — solve

The solver converges in 3 iterations, to a remaining imbalance of 1.9×10⁻¹⁶ m³/s.

The ring is symmetrical. You can check the flows without a solver. Total demand is 3 L/s. Both sides have the same resistance, so each carries 1.5 L/s. The middle outflow sits at the meeting point. It takes 0.5 L/s from each side. The solver returns this.

The iterations, one by one

Three things in that table are worth reading carefully.

Continuity is satisfied from the first iteration. The imbalance column is at round-off from the start and stays there. That is not the solver converging — it is a property of the method. Continuity is a linear constraint, so the linear system solved at each step enforces it exactly; only the energy equation is non-linear, and only it has to be iterated. A GGA run whose imbalance is not negligible on the first iteration is reporting a singular or disconnected network, not a solve in progress.

The convergence is quadratic. Each step is a Newton step, so the error is roughly squared each time. The largest flow change falls 2.90 → 0.046 → 0.0000022 L/s: five orders of magnitude across two iterations. This is why the iteration cap of 100 is generous rather than tight — a network that has not converged in a dozen iterations usually has something wrong with it rather than something difficult about it.

The first iteration overshoots. The ring leg reaches 1.545939 L/s before settling back to 1.499967. Starting from a seed flow three orders of magnitude below the answer, the linearised slope at that point is far too steep, and the first correction goes past. That is normal and self-correcting; it is also why the flow-change test exists rather than judging on head alone.

The tolerances the run above was judged against are the ones in section 6.2: a largest head change below 1 mm, a nodal imbalance below 0.01 L/s, and a flow change below either 10⁻⁸ m³/s or 0.01% of the largest flow in the network. SUGGESTION:

Look at the Critical Path section of the calculation sheet. It lists the sections from the source to the index terminal, and the friction, static and residual pressure along it. The pump duty is the total of that path.

Which circuit is the index in a closed system

An open system has a plain answer: the terminal with the least residual pressure. A closed circuit does not, and the reason is worth stating because it rules out the obvious method.

The total resistance around every circuit is the same. The head changes around any closed loop sum to zero, so for every loop through the pump, friction + static equals the pump head exactly. Ranking circuits by their total loss cannot separate them — they all tie.

What differs is how that total is split. Every circuit spends the pump head on pipework plus its own terminal, so the index is the circuit where the pipework takes the most — and therefore the one whose terminal is left with the least differential across its own branch. That is the figure a commissioning engineer reads off a pair of gauges, and it is what the program ranks by.

NOTE:

Ranking by how well each terminal is served — its flow against its rated flow — works only where nothing is controlling it. A terminal with a control valve is driven to its setpoint by that valve, so on a run of identical terminals none is starved and the small differences left between them are the valves' one-percent travel resolution rather than the pipework. On a fourteen-terminal example the spread was 0.57%, and the terminal it picked was the least remote on the run.

NOTE:

Every value in this worked example is independently recomputed and compared against the engine output in Engine Validation.

Choosing the path by hand

You can select the two ends of the critical path yourself, on the CALCULATION tab. One of the two nodes must be on a pump.

The program then reports the circuit between the two nodes and back to the pump. It does not look for an index circuit. Equipment on the route is included, because the route is the one you selected.

SUGGESTION:

Use a manual path to verify a result. The automatic path is a correct choice, but it is made from numbers that are not on the sheet. When many circuits are almost the same, select the run you want and compare the total against your own calculation.

7. Pumps

7.1 Modes

ModeWhat the program does
AutoIn DESIGN, raises or lowers the head until the worst-served terminal has exactly its required pressure. The duty can come down as well as up.
ManualYou state the flow and the head. The program does not change them.
CurveYou give H against Q, from the manufacturer. In SIMULATE the operating point is read from this curve.

A safety margin, as a percentage, can be added to the calculated duty. The margin is applied to the reported pump selection only. It is not applied inside the solve, so it cannot change the calculated operating point.

SIMULATION requires a curve. If no manufacturer data is pasted, the program generates one from the design point (section 7.2), so a pump sized in DESIGN is always ready to simulate.

7.2 The curve equation

A pump curve is stored as a falling power law:

Pump head H = H0 − a · Qb

where H is in metres, Q in m³/s, H0 is the shutoff head (head at zero flow), and a and b shape the curve. The solver uses this form because its derivative is simple:

Slope dH/dQ = −b · a · Qb−1

This is the slope the Newton iteration uses. For the default exponent b = 2 this simplifies to dH/dQ = −2aQ.

Generated curve (default)

When no manufacturer data is available, the program generates a curve from three stated points:

  • shutoff — zero flow, at a percentage of the duty head (default 140%),
  • duty — the design flow and head,
  • runout — a percentage of the duty flow (default 150%) at a percentage of the duty head (default 65%).

The defaults follow the NFPA 20 fire-pump envelope. All three percentages are editable in SETTINGS. The program then fits the stored power law to the three points. Three parameters fitted to three points reproduce the stated points essentially exactly (the fit reports its own deviation, typically below 10⁻⁶ m).

NOTE:

Earlier versions generated the curve from the EPANET single-point assumption: shutoff at 133% of duty head, runout at 200% of duty flow. That was removed. The arithmetic was correct, but the feature presented one manufacturer-agnostic guess as though it were the answer, and it cannot reach shapes that are actually specified, such as the NFPA 20 envelope above.

Pasted manufacturer data

When manufacturer data is pasted (multiple Q, H points), the program fits H = H0 − a·Qb by least squares. The exponent b is swept from 1.2 to 3.0, and H0 and a are solved exactly at each candidate. The best b is then refined by narrowing the sweep around the winner. The program reports R² and the maximum deviation, so a curve that does not fit this form is visible rather than silently wrong.

The Generic Pump Curve tool (TOOLS window) fits an exact quadratic h(q) = a + b·q + c·q² through three stated duties, using Newton's divided differences. That quadratic is an interpolation: it passes through all three points exactly. The solver's own power-law form has no linear term and cannot hold a quadratic exactly, so paste the three defining points into the pump — the power-law fit reproduces the stated duties, which are the contractual ones. The full 0–150% table spreads the error more evenly but moves the stated points. See the TOOLS documentation.

7.3 Affinity laws — variable speed

A pump running at a fraction s of rated speed follows the affinity laws:

Affinity scaling Q ∝ N     H ∝ N²

Hs(Q) = s² · H(Q / s)

Substituting into the stored curve produces a new curve in the same form:

H0' = s² · H0
a' = a · s(2−b)
b' = b

At b = 2 the exponent term is s⁰ = 1, so a is unchanged and the curve simply drops. This is the standard VSD family. The duty point rides the affinity parabola to (s·Qd, s²·Hd).

NOTE:

The program does not carry a power curve, so there is nothing to be wrong about. The affinity laws are exact for the ideal machine and are the basis of every published VSD family.

7.4 How the solver uses the pump

The energy equation along a pump link is:

Hfrom − Hto = −Hpump(Q)

The pump adds head. The term is negative because flow goes from low head to high head across the pump. For a fixed-head pump, Hpump is constant. For a curve, the solver finds the operating point where the curve meets the system. It does this for the whole network at once. No separate system-curve intersection is needed or wanted. With several pumps and rings there is no single scalar system curve to intersect.

The derivative dH/dQ vanishes at shutoff for b > 1. This is the same singularity a fixed-head pump has. The slope is floored at a minimum (1.0 m/(m³/s)) to keep the Newton step finite.

Automatic sizing (DESIGN)

In DESIGN, automatic sizing adjusts the pump head until the worst-served terminal has exactly its required pressure. The head can come down as well as up. A linear droop is imposed on the resulting head — H = Hd + k(Qref − Q) — so the sizer can always reach its target. The slope k defaults to Hd/Qref.

Synced pumps are sized as one machine

Pumps that are synced act as one set, so the leader states the selection and the followers take it: the sizing mode, the design flow and head, and the curve. Only the leader is sized. This keeps a ganged set from holding two different duties while it runs at one speed.

A synced pump that is switched off keeps its own On/Off state. Standby is a separate decision from selection, and a synced spare still carries the leader’s curve so it is ready to run.

NOTE:

On a synced pump the panel greys the design flow and the design pressure, shows the leader’s values in them, and marks the flow row “(Synced with …)”.

7.5 Control

A pump can follow a sensor. Link the pump to a pressure, flow or temperature sensor and give the sensor a setpoint. In SIMULATE the program adjusts the pump speed until the sensor reads its setpoint. If the pump reaches a limit before it reaches the setpoint, the program says so.

NOTE:

In DESIGN the demands set the flow, and automatic sizing sets the duty. A controller cannot also set them: two controllers on one actuator have no single answer. In SIMULATE the flow is free to respond, so the controller has something to act on.

8. Equipment

Equipment is any in-line device that adds or removes heat, or drops pressure without a thermal effect. Three types share one hydraulic resistance model. They differ in their thermal behaviour.

8.1 Hydraulic resistance

Every piece of equipment carries a pressure drop rated at a design flow. The solver converts this to a quadratic resistance:

Equipment resistance r = ΔPrated / (ρ · g · Qrated²)

hloss = r · Q²

where ΔPrated is in Pa, Qrated in m³/s, and ρ is the fluid density. The exponent is always 2 (Darcy-like), regardless of the pipe friction method.

An integrated control valve (ICV) on a coil is in series with the coil on the same link. The rated pressure drop is the total for the two together. The valve at full travel is taken out of the rating to give the coil its own resistance, then the valve is added back at its actual position:

Coil alone rcoil = requip − rvalve(Kv,ICV, 100%)

The link rtotal = rcoil + rvalve(Kv,ICV, opening)

At full travel the two cancel, so the link drops exactly the rated pressure at the rated flow. This is what makes a differential pressure setpoint work: a sensor placed across the machine reads the coil and its valve, because that is where the tappings go. If the rating were the coil alone, a setpoint equal to the rating could never deliver the rated flow — it would be short by whatever the valve drops.

NOTE:

A drawn valve is not treated this way. It is separate pipework and is charged on top of the rating. The same Kv therefore gives a different answer depending on whether the valve is part of the machine or drawn in the branch, because those are two different plants. A machine's quoted pressure drop normally includes the valve it ships with.

IMPORTANT:

If the valve at full travel drops more than the whole rated pressure, no coil is left. The coil is held at zero, the machine becomes its own valve, and the program reports ICV_EXCEEDS_PD. Raise the valve Kv, or raise the rated pressure drop.

Equipment that is switched off is omitted from the network entirely — a genuine break, not a bypass.

Which opening the two modes use

An integrated valve is Auto or Manual.

Manual is a balancing valve you set yourself. Its position is an input and is read in both modes, exactly like a drawn valve with no control link. At 100% it is treated as no valve at all, so a coil you do not want a valve on is a manual valve left at full travel.

Auto is a control valve built into the machine, modulating to hold that machine's own design ΔT. It is in the circuit in both modes and is charged for in both. The opening is where they differ, and the rule is the same for an integrated valve and for a drawn globe valve carrying a control link.

ModeOpening usedWhy
Design Full travel, always A design calculation is at design conditions. The position a valve comes to rest at is a commissioning result, not a design input, so the design solve charges for the valve wide open and takes no notice of the number in the panel.
Simulation Whatever the control loop settles on Here the position is the answer being sought. The loop modulates the valve to hold its setpoint and writes the result back.

A valve with no control link — a drawn valve you have not linked, or an integrated valve set to Manual — is a balancing valve. Its position is a decision somebody made, so it is read in both modes exactly as set.

NOTE:

This matters most when identifying the index circuit. On a run of identical terminals, each holding its own setpoint, every terminal is driven to design flow and the small differences left between them are the valves' one-percent travel resolution rather than the pipework. Ranking such a system by how well each terminal is served would report whichever valve happened to round down, which can be the least remote terminal on the run. With the valves at full travel the design ranking is the pipework, which is what an index circuit is.

8.2 Thermal behaviour

Temperature is transported by the water. The thermal solve runs after the hydraulic solve and reads its flows. Nothing here feeds back into the hydraulics. Fluid properties are fixed at one temperature. The three thermal phenomena are:

Mixing at a junction

Rule of mixtures, mass-weighted:

Tmix = Σ(ṁi · Ti) / Σ(ṁi)

Weighted by mass flow rather than volume because it is energy that mixes; with one fluid and constant Cp the Cp cancels and this is exact.

Pipe heat loss or gain

For constant ambient temperature and a constant overall heat-loss coefficient, the outlet temperature has a closed form:

Pipe outlet temperature Tout = Tamb + (Tin − Tamb) · exp(−U' · L / (ṁ · Cp))

where U' is the loss per metre per kelvin, from the insulation geometry. This is exponential, not linear. The driving difference shrinks as the water approaches ambient. On a long run at low flow, a linear model can walk the temperature straight past ambient and out the other side. This form cannot. It approaches ambient and stops.

U' is computed from the insulation and surface film:

R' = ln(ro/ri) / (2πk) + 1 / (2π · ro · ho)
U' = 1 / R'

where ri is the pipe outside radius. Insulation sits on the pipe, not in the bore. ro = ri + insulation thickness. k is the insulation conductivity. ho is the outside surface coefficient (default 8 W/(m²·K)). Setting ho = 0 makes the pipe adiabatic — this is the way to model a genuinely sealed circuit.

Two thermal types are distinguished by what you know at design:

Sign is inferred, never selected. A setpoint below the inlet is cooling. A chilled-water coil is positive — the water gets warmer — even though the room is being cooled.

Source / sink — leaving temperature

The machine holds its leaving temperature by modulating duty. The duty it needs at the current flow and inlet is:

Required duty Qneed = ṁ · Cp · (Tset − Tin)

This is signed. It is negative for cooling (Tset < Tin) and positive for heating. The machine is limited by capacity:

  • If the capacity is blank, the machine is unlimited.
  • If the required duty and the signed capacity have opposite signs, the machine cannot work that way. It delivers nothing, and the program reports "Capacity (wrong direction)".
  • If the magnitude of the required duty exceeds the ceiling below, the duty is capped there and the program reports "Capacity".

The outlet is Tout = Tin + Qdelivered / (ṁ · Cp), in the correct direction. The temperature limit (Tlimit) is not applied to a source/sink: whether a leaving temperature is physically achievable is a judgement the engineer makes.

Heat exchanger — stated load

The duty is the stated quantity. Temperature follows:

Exchanger outlet temperature Tout = Tin + duty / (ṁ · Cp)

Limited by:

  • Design ΔT (ΔTmax): if the actual ΔT would exceed the design value, the duty is capped.
  • Temperature limit (Tlimit): the machine cannot take the fluid past this physical limit. The limit is applied on whichever side the machine works from.

Overload capacity

Plant does not stop dead at its nameplate. A machine may run a stated percentage past it before the duty is held:

Ceiling |qmax| × (1 + Overload capacity / 100)

Overload capacity is on the THERMAL tab under Temperature Range, and is 10% by default. Set it to 0 to hold a machine at exactly its rating.

NOTE:

The pressure drop is not part of this. Equipment resistance is fixed by the rated point, so a machine carrying more than its rated flow pays the square law: 10% more flow costs 21% more head, whatever the capacity is doing.

SUGGESTION:

Two different things are reported, and only for capacity. A machine over its nameplate that is still holding its setpoint is overloaded — the allowance in use, and the machine is named with its percentage. A machine pinned at the ceiling and still short of its setpoint is limited, which is a failure. A machine held by its Design ΔT, or working inside its rating, is not reported at all. A machine asked to work in the wrong direction is always reported, because that is a data error rather than an operating condition.

NOTE:

This matters most on a plant of several machines sharing one loop. When they are near full load, a fraction of a per cent of difference in the flow each receives is enough to put one of them over while the others sit under. Treating the capacity as a hard wall made that one machine clamp, which dragged the loop temperature up until the rest clamped too. The allowance lets it carry the small excess, and the report names the single machine that is doing so.

There is no capacity limit. The duty is the stated quantity.

Adiabatic

A filter, strainer, or flow meter: real pipework with a real pressure drop and no thermal properties. The water leaves as it arrived. This is a type rather than a zero duty. "No thermal behaviour" and "a duty that happens to be zero" are different statements. Only the first hides the thermal fields and refuses to be a control target.

Synced heat exchangers

Heat exchangers can be synced to each other. A synced coil copies the leader’s part load, and also its duty, rated flow and rated pressure drop — a sync group is a set of identical machines. Sync a group only where the machines really are the same selection. Where the coils differ, set their part loads separately.

Load percentage

A loadPct scales what the machine is asked to do without touching the design figure. This is a way of asking "what if this coil were at 40%?" without losing the number the machine is scheduled at. If absent, the design figure is the answer. For a source/sink the percentage also scales the capacity, so a 50% load on a machine with a 100 kW capacity can use up to 50 kW.

8.4 The thermal solve is exact

Every relation is affine in temperature:

PhenomenonRelationForm
MixingT = Σ(ṁiTi) / ΣṁiLinear
Pipe lossTout = Tamb + (Tin − Tamb) · e−xAffine, e−x fixed
EquipmentTout = Tin + ΔT  or  Tin + Q/CAffine

The whole network is one linear system A·T = b. It is solved exactly in one pass. Equipment clamps are piecewise linear. Which branch applies depends on the inlet temperature, which is what the solve produces. The active set is frozen, the system is solved, the set is re-computed from the answer, and it repeats until nothing changes — typically two or three passes. A pass cap exists so a pathological model reports an error rather than hanging.

Nodes with no arriving flow (dead legs, shut branches, standby equipment) cannot use the mixing relation. They are tied to the temperature of the connected water by breadth-first search, not reset to the supply temperature. A genuinely isolated island falls back to the seed temperature.

9. Outflows

An outflow is a demand node. Water leaves the system there. The solver treats it differently depending on the mode.

9.1 DESIGN — stated flow

In DESIGN, each outflow states a required flow Qd and a required pressure ΔPd. The solver treats the flow as a fixed demand at the node:

Node continuity Σ Qin − Σ Qout = Qd

The required pressure sets the pump duty — it is not used in the solve itself, only in sizing the pump to satisfy the worst terminal.

9.2 SIMULATION — pressure-dependent terminal

In SIMULATION, the outflow becomes a short link to a virtual discharge node. The discharge node is pinned at the outflow's own elevation (zero gauge). The link carries a quadratic resistance derived from the design point:

Terminal resistance K = Qd / √(ΔPd)

r = ΔPd / (ρ · g · Qd²)

Flow through that link is what the terminal actually delivers:

Q = K · √P     or equivalently     Q = √(P / r) / √(ρg)

A fixture therefore discharges more water where the pressure is high, and less where it is short. This is correct behaviour and it is what you use the simulation to find.

IMPORTANT:

An outflow with zero required pressure has no characteristic K = Q/√P and cannot be simulated. The smallest meaningful value is 100 Pa (0.1 kPa).

9.3 Domestic water fixtures

In the plumbing discipline, an outflow is a fixture. Its design flow comes from fixture units (section 13). In SIMULATION it behaves identically to a generic outflow: Q = K·√P, with K derived from the fixture's undiversified flow and required pressure.

A setpoint can be a limit

Every setpoint carries a comparator, and the default is SET — hold this value, which is what setpoints have always done.

ComparatorMeaningWhere the device sits when it is not biting
SETHold the value. The controller drives the reading to the setpoint from either side.Wherever holding it requires.
MINA floor. The controller acts only when the reading falls below the setpoint.At its minimum — a bypass valve sits shut.
MAXA ceiling. The mirror of MIN: act only when the reading rises above it.At full travel.

The worked case is a bypass valve holding a chiller's minimum flow. Put a flow sensor on the chiller, set it to the minimum flow with MIN, and link the bypass valve to it. While the load is high the coils are open, the chiller is above its minimum, and the bypass stays shut. As the coils close down the chiller flow falls; the bypass opens exactly as far as it must to hold the floor, and no further.

NOTE:

SET is not a substitute for MIN here. Asked to hold flow equal to the minimum, the same bypass valve cannot reduce the flow that far while the system is busy, so it reports the setpoint as lost and is parked wide open — the opposite of what the valve should be doing. A floor and a target are different instructions.

A limit is searched differently from a target. There is no point where the error crosses zero: it is zero across everything that satisfies the limit, so the answer is the boundary of that region — for MIN, its lowest end, because a device that is not needed should be doing nothing.

10. Sources

A source is a boundary condition: it states the pressure (or head) the water arrives at. In an open system a source is where water enters the network.

10.1 Hydraulic

The source sets a fixed head at its node:

Hsource = z + Psource / (ρ · g)

where z is the node elevation and Psource is the gauge pressure at the connection point (for example, mains pressure). The solver uses this as a known head — the same role a reservoir plays in EPANET.

An off-screen or atmospheric source at zero gauge pressure sets H = z.

A closed loop (a chilled-water ring or heating circuit) has no source. One node is pinned instead, at its own elevation, to act as the pressure datum. The program chooses a node at the pump suction. This fixes the pressure level without injecting or removing any water.

10.2 Thermal

A source introduces water at a stated temperature. It is a stream, not a hard pin. It mixes with whatever arrives at the node, carrying only the mass it actually introduces.

Source mixing Tnode = (ṁpipes · Tmixed + ṁsource · Tsource) / (ṁpipes + ṁsource)

On a branch-end source, the source brings in all the flow. The node is at the source temperature and nothing changes. Teed into a live main, only the make-up mixes. The rest of the water flows past.

A source moving no water at all still states a temperature. It is a stated boundary that happens to be idle, not a dead leg guessing from its neighbour.

NOTE:

A closed loop has no source of water, so it has no source of temperature. One node is pinned instead. The pin sits at the outlet of whatever removes the most heat — the plant. Its leaving temperature is the number an engineer quotes as the flow temperature. If the temperature field has no unique solution (an adiabatic loop with nothing tying it to ambient), the program reports an error.

11. Valves

Valves are sized by flow coefficient rather than equivalent length. The solver charges them as a quadratic resistance: h = r·Q².

11.1 Kv and Cv

Flow coefficient Kv = flow in m³/h that produces a 1 bar drop across the valve
Cv = flow in US gpm that produces a 1 psi drop. Cv ≈ 1.156 · Kv.

Pressure drop follows the square law:

ΔP [bar] = (Q [m³/h] / Kv)²

Converted to SI head-basis resistance for h = r·|Q|·Q, Q in m³/s:

Valve resistance r = 105 · 3600² / (Kv² · ρ · g)

A shut valve uses r = 1012. This is high enough to pass no meaningful flow, but finite so the solver matrix stays non-singular.

where A is the pipe bore area. The default Kv values are derived, not manufacturer data. They are here so the model runs with sensible figures and are explicitly intended to be replaced by product data.

11.3 How Kv changes with opening

A valve does not open in proportion to its travel. The flow coefficient at a part-open position is a fraction of the full-open value:

Effective coefficient Kv,eff(x) = Kv,full · f(x)

x is the opening from 0 to 100%. f(x) is the characteristic of the valve — the fraction of full coefficient it passes at that travel. The program stores f(x) as a table for each valve type and interpolates between the tabulated points in a straight line. Outside the table it holds the end value.

What the solver actually uses is the resistance, and the resistance is the square of the inverse of the coefficient (section 11.1). So a small coefficient at part travel becomes a large resistance:

Resistance against travel r(x) = rfull / f(x)²

This square is why the shape of f(x) matters so much. The table below gives f(x) for the two adjustable valves, and beside each the resistance it produces as a multiple of the full-open resistance.

So a control valve rated Kv = 50 passes an effective Kv of 50 × 0.40 = 20 at 70% travel, and 50 × 0.15 = 7.5 at half travel.

NOTE:

Equal percentage is chosen so the valve keeps control across its whole range when it sits in series with fixed pipe. As the valve closes, more of the loop's pressure falls across it, which would make a linear valve act mostly near the shut end. The rising shape cancels that, so a given step of travel gives a similar change in flow wherever the valve is. This is standard control-valve theory (ISA-75.01 / IEC 60534).

11.4 Check valve

A check valve blocks reverse flow. Direction is only known after the solve. This uses a two-pass approach:

  1. Solve with the geometric estimate of flow direction.
  2. If the upstream head is lower than the downstream head, the valve is holding shut (r = 1012).
  3. Solve again.

The test is on head, not flow. Testing flow oscillates forever: shut the valve because flow was negative. The next pass reports ~zero flow, which is not negative, so it reopens. The adverse head difference is stable because it persists while the valve is held shut.

12. Thermal summary

The thermal module is a single linear system. It is solved in one pass, plus two or three active-set iterations for equipment clamps. The three phenomena — mixing, pipe heat loss, and equipment duty — are all affine in temperature.

12.1 Energy balance

At steady state, what goes in must come out:

Σ qequip + Σ qpipe + qsource − qboundary = 0

For a closed circuit the boundary term is zero. No water enters or leaves. For an open circuit, water carries energy across the boundary. The residual is reported separately from the link duties.

12.2 Reference temperature

Something must be known. Without it, every temperature floats. In priority order:

  1. A source states the temperature of the water it introduces. This is stream mixing, not a hard pin.
  2. A pinned datum at the outlet of the equipment moving the most heat. This is used only when no source introduces water and the loop is adiabatic (no pipe heat exchange). The pin holds its stated temperature whatever arrives, and the energy it absorbs is reported as a heat imbalance.
  3. If neither is needed (the loop is coupled to ambient through pipe heat loss), the system finds its own level against ambient.

12.3 Pumps and valves

Pumps and valves pass temperature straight through. A pump does put shaft work into the water. At typical duties that is hundredths of a kelvin. Stating it would imply a precision the rest of the thermal module does not have.

13. Domestic water

A domestic water file is a separate discipline. It is sized from fixture units, to the International Plumbing Code 2018, Appendix E. The network solver is not used to size it.

13.1 From fixtures to a design flow

  1. Each fixture carries a number of cold-water fixture units, from IPC Table E103.3(2). A fixture unit is a measure of load, not a flow.

  2. The program adds the fixture units of everything downstream of a pipe.

  3. It converts that total to a probable flow through a demand curve, IPC Table E103.3(3). There are two curves: one for systems served predominantly by flush tanks, and one for systems served predominantly by flushometer (flush) valves. Select the curve on the HYDRAULIC tab. This curve is sub-additive. 100 fixture units draw far less than 100 times the flow of one. The fixtures do not all run at once.

  4. Any generic outflow downstream is added on top, without diversity. A continuous draw — a hose, a process supply — is not a fixture unit and does not diversify.

Design flow in a pipe Q = fuToFlow( Σ fixture units downstream ) + Σ generic flow downstream
NOTE:

Because the demand curve is sub-additive, the flow in a main is less than the total of the branches it feeds. This is correct. It is diversity, not an error in continuity.

13.2 The network must be a tree

"Downstream" is only defined when there is one path from the source to each fixture. The program therefore requires a tree: one source, no loops. If it finds a loop, more than one source, or no source, it reports the problem instead of estimating an answer.

13.3 Fixture flow and pressure

Each fixture also carries an undiversified flow and a required flow pressure, from IPC Table 604.3. These are the values at the outlet when that fixture runs on its own. They are used to size a booster and to simulate.

Where a fixture is not listed in Table 604.3, the value is estimated from similar fixtures. Estimated values are shown in red on the HYDRAULIC tab. All values are editable. A plumbing outflow carries both a fixture (for fixture units) and a supply outlet (for undiversified flow and pressure); they are chosen independently.

13.4 Booster duty

A booster must satisfy the most remote fixture while that fixture is running, at its full outlet flow. Everything behind it is still a population, and still diversifies.

Duty flow Q = undiversified flow of the index fixture
  + fuToFlow( total fixture units − fixture units of the index fixture )
  + generic flow served

The index fixture is the one with the least margin between the pressure delivered to it and the pressure it requires. This is the same definition the Critical Path uses, so the calculation sheet and the pump agree.

The duty head is the head that brings the index fixture to exactly its required pressure. The forward pass is linear in pump head, so this is exact in one pass. The duty comes down as readily as it goes up.

13.5 Simulation

DESIGN sizes the pipe. SIMULATE tells you what the system delivers.

In SIMULATE each fixture that is switched On becomes a pressure-dependent terminal. It behaves exactly as a hydronic terminal does. Its K comes from its own design point:

Fixture characteristic Q = K · √P     K = Qdesign / √Pdesign

A fixture therefore discharges more water where the pressure is high, and less where it is short. This is correct behaviour. This is what you use the simulation to find.

SUGGESTION:

One source, one pipe, one lavatory. Double the supply pressure and the draw increases by √2, as the law requires.

300 kPa → 0.1177 L/s
600 kPa → 0.1665 L/s

0.1665 / 0.1177 = 1.4142    (√2 = 1.4142)

You decide which fixtures are open, with the On/Off switch on each outflow. A multiple selection can be switched together. The load case is yours to choose. The program does not choose it for you.

IMPORTANT:

A fixed-speed booster develops more head as the draw falls. The highest pressure in a domestic system is therefore at its quietest, not its busiest. The program warns above a static pressure limit, which defaults to 552 kPa (80 psi — the IPC 604.8 threshold for requiring a pressure-reducing valve) and is editable on the HYDRAULIC tab.

15. Limits of the method

These are known. Read them before you rely on a result.

IMPORTANT:

Straight-pipe friction is validated. Tee losses are not.

A tee is charged as a "run" or a "branch" from its geometry and flow direction. Real tee loss depends on whether the flow divides or combines, and above all on the ratio of branch flow to combined flow. K varies over that ratio by more than a factor of ten. One flat coefficient cannot represent it. The program therefore charges combining tees the same as dividing tees.

The effect is small on a network whose losses are mostly straight pipe. It is larger on short, heavily branched pipework.

IMPORTANT:

Equivalent-length and K table values are linearly interpolated between the tabulated sizes. Outside the table they are clamped. A pipe below DN25 is charged the DN25 value, which is conservative. The NFPA 13 set has no straight-through tee row, so that row comes from the Carrier table. Verify the numbers against your project standard.

IMPORTANT:

ASHRAE publishes the uncertainty of its K values. Threaded 90° elbows vary ±20% above DN50 and ±40% below it; flanged check valves range +200/−80%. Use manufacturer data for critical selections.

IMPORTANT:

Density and viscosity are entered, not calculated from the temperature. A circuit running at 6 °C with 20 °C properties entered will use the 20 °C properties.

IMPORTANT:

Velocity pressure is neglected. This is appropriate for plumbing and hydronic work, not for fire protection design.
